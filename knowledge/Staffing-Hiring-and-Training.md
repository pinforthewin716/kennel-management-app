# Staffing, Hiring, and Training for a Boarding & Daycare Facility

Reference for building and running the team at a dog boarding, daycare, and grooming facility (Grayslake, IL). Covers roles, staff-to-dog ratios, hiring and screening, onboarding and training curriculum, scheduling, retention, and certifications. People are the single biggest driver of safety outcomes in a kennel — most incidents trace back to under-staffing, under-training, or a missed body-language signal.

---

## 1. Roles

| Role | Core responsibilities | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Daycare / play attendant** | Supervises group play, reads body language, breaks up tension before it escalates, runs rotations, manages water/rest | Highest-responsibility floor role. Needs the most behavior training. |
| **Kennel technician** | Boarding care — feeding, medication, cleaning/disinfection, potty breaks, wellness checks, overnight monitoring | Owns the biosecurity routine (see Health & Safety doc). |
| **Groomer / bather** | Bathing, grooming, handling for nail/ear care; flags skin, lumps, parasites at intake | Often a separate revenue line; may be 1099 or employed. Needs safe-restraint skill. |
| **Front desk / client care** | Check-in/out, vaccine-proof verification, intake forms, scheduling, payments, owner comms | Gatekeeper for vaccine compliance and accurate records. |
| **Manager / lead** | Scheduling, training, escalation, incident handling, quality and safety oversight, hiring | Should hold or pursue a manager-level certification (CPACM). |
| **Owner / operator** | Standards, licensing, vet relationship, financials, culture | Operator-level certification (CPACO) signals trust to clients. |

Small facilities combine roles (one person doing kennel + daycare). That's fine **as long as ratios and supervision coverage are never violated** — a combined role can't simultaneously supervise a play group and clean boarding runs.

---

## 2. Staff-to-Dog Ratios

The **IBPSA** guideline is a maximum of **1 staff per 10–15 dogs in active supervised group play.**

- **1:10 (conservative end)** — high-energy groups, dogs new to the facility, mixed-size play, or less-experienced staff. Most well-run facilities operate **closer to 1:10** during active play.
- **1:15 (ceiling)** — only for familiar, compatible, well-managed groups with good sightlines and experienced staff.
- **Rest periods** (dogs crated or in separated rest areas) need less active supervision, so ratios can flex during downtime — but boarding still requires regular wellness rounds, including overnight coverage or monitoring.

**Practical rules:**
- Never run a play group with a single staffer if a second can't respond fast — a fight or medical emergency needs two hands.
- Size the group to the **weakest sightline**, not the floor's square footage. If you can't see every dog, the group is too big.
- Factor temperament and size mixing: small-dog and large-dog groups should generally be separated, each with their own supervision.

## 3. Hiring & Screening

**Where to source:**
- Local sources: veterinary tech programs, community colleges with animal-science tracks, grooming schools, 4-H / dog-sport communities, rescue/shelter volunteers (already comfortable handling dogs).
- Industry boards: IBPSA, PACCC networks, pet-care job boards, and word-of-mouth referrals from existing staff (often the best hires).
- Local channels for the Grayslake/Lake County area: nearby vet clinics, dog-training clubs, and community boards.

**Screen specifically for dog-handling aptitude — not just a résumé:**
- **Working interview / paid trial shift** on the floor. This is the single most predictive screen. Watch how they move around dogs, whether dogs settle or tense around them, and whether they stay calm when play gets loud.
- Test for **body-language literacy**: show video or live play and ask what they see. Can they spot a stressed dog?
- Probe judgment with scenarios: "Two dogs start humping/escalating — what do you do?" "A dog won't eat — what do you do?"
- Reference checks for reliability and prior animal-care work.
- **Background check** appropriate to a role handling client property/pets and (for some) keys/cash.
- Physical reality check: the job is on-your-feet, lifting, cleaning, and occasionally getting knocked around. Be honest about it.

**Red flags:** discomfort or fear around large/excited dogs, rough/punitive handling instinct, can't take direction, unreliable attendance in the trial.

## 4. Onboarding & Training Curriculum

New hires should never run a play group alone until they've completed structured onboarding and been observed. Build a written curriculum so training isn't ad hoc.

### Core modules
1. **Canine body language & stress signals** — the foundation. Reading ears, tail, posture, hackles, lip-licking, yawning, whale-eye, "tapping out" of play. Recognizing healthy play vs. brewing conflict.
2. **Group-play supervision & rotations** — how to position for sightlines, when and how to **interrupt** unwanted behavior (and which behaviors to interrupt), managing arousal, structuring rest, and right-sizing/sorting groups by temperament and energy.
3. **Temperament testing / intake assessment** — how to evaluate a new dog before it joins a group: energy, social skills, triggers; which group it fits or whether it's a daycare candidate at all.
4. **Safe handling & restraint** — leashing, moving dogs between spaces, safe break-ups, bite-avoidance.
5. **Emergency response** — fight/bite protocol, **pet first aid & CPR**, heatstroke recognition and cooling, choke/bloat red flags, the facility's emergency-vet plan and when to act without waiting for the owner. (See Health & Safety doc.)
6. **Health & biosecurity** — cleaning/disinfection at the right dilution and contact time, spotting illness (CIRDC cough, GI signs, appetite changes), isolation procedure, parasite checks.
7. **Feeding & medication** — reading feeding instructions, logging meals, the double-check med-administration routine, recordkeeping.
8. **Documentation & software** — using the kennel app for logs, incident reports, and owner notes.
9. **Customer service & comms** — calm, clear owner updates; how to report an incident to an owner.

### Method
- **Shadow → supervised → independent.** New staff observe, then work alongside an experienced handler, then take a group only after a lead signs off.
- **Ongoing, not one-and-done:** monthly walkthroughs on a specific behavior topic, **case reviews after every incident**, and structured observation time for newer staff. The best facilities never stop training.
- Document completion of each module per employee (also useful for liability and licensing).

## 5. Scheduling & Time-Clock Needs

- **Coverage-driven scheduling:** build the schedule around the ratio and the daily curve — heavy at drop-off/pick-up and active play, lighter overnight. Boarding adds early-morning, late-evening, **weekend, and holiday** coverage that daycare-only operations don't have.
- **Overnight care:** decide and staff your boarding model — on-site overnight staff vs. scheduled check rounds — and make it explicit to owners.
- **Time-clock / labor tracking:** use a time-clock system (many pet-care software suites include scheduling + time tracking) to control labor cost, prevent under-staffing a shift, and document coverage. Labor is the largest controllable expense — track scheduled-vs-actual against booked dog count.
- **Don't schedule below ratio to save money.** An under-staffed play floor is where bites and escapes happen; one incident costs far more than the saved wage.

---

## 6. Retention

Pet care has high turnover; the cost of constant re-hiring and re-training is a hidden margin killer, and an experienced handler who knows the dogs is a safety asset.

- **Pay and recognition** competitive for the local market; reward tenure and certification.
- **Invest in training and pay for certifications** — staff stay where they grow, and certified staff are safer and a marketing point.
- **Realistic workload and ratios** — burnout comes from chronic under-staffing. Protect breaks; the job is physically and emotionally demanding.
- **Culture and autonomy** — people who love dogs stay for a workplace that respects the work and listens after incidents instead of blaming.
- **Clear advancement path:** attendant → lead → manager, tied to certification levels.

## 7. Certifications

Certifications raise safety, reduce liability, and are a genuine marketing differentiator to owners choosing where to leave their dog.

### PACCC — Professional Animal Care Certification Council (the main industry credential)
Three tiers, each valid **3 years** with continuing-education (CEU) renewal:
- **CPACP — Provider** (front-line staff): 125-question / 2.5-hr exam (~$385); 20 CEUs to renew. Covers health, meds, first aid/CPR, cleaning/disinfecting, fights & bites, documentation, play, temperament, behavior.
- **CPACM — Manager:** ~$485; 30 CEUs to renew.
- **CPACO — Operator** (owner/operator): 175-question / 4-hr exam (~$585); 30 CEUs to renew. Covers the full operations scope including health, meds, first aid/CPR, cleaning/disinfecting.

### Pet First Aid & CPR (require for all hands-on staff)
- Non-negotiable for anyone on the floor. Available via PACCC CEU courses, the Red Cross, and providers like ProPetHero/Project Pup. Keep every hands-on staffer current.

### Behavior / training credentials (for play-group leads)
- **CPDT-KA** (Certified Professional Dog Trainer) or equivalent for staff who independently run group play or do temperament assessments — deeper behavior knowledge than a general care cert.

### Continuing education
- **IBPSA** offers CEU courses (e.g., a multi-CEU dog daycare staff-training course) that count toward PACCC renewal — a cost-effective way to keep the team trained and certifications current at the same time.

**Recommended baseline for this facility:** every hands-on hire works toward **CPACP + current Pet First Aid/CPR**; play-group leads add a behavior credential; the manager holds **CPACM** and the owner **CPACO**. It's both a safety floor and a "our staff are certified" trust signal in local marketing.
